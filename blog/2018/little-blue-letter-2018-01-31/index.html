Cyber pirates
The shipping industry carries around 90% of world trade. Almost any product you can think of follows a well-oiled distribution chain: factory, truck, ship, truck, consumer. Any disruption to this chain results in significant economic loss for companies, and ultimately the customer. While maritime piracy is traditionally enemy number one, costing up to $16 billion per year, new technologies are bringing more advanced threats.

Last year, the NotPetya ransomware attack caused chaos at the Port of New York and New Jersey. Trucks waited up to 8 hours to pick up goods as one of the world's busiest ports was forced to revert to manual systems for managing incoming cargo. The episode was a jarring wake-up call to the shipping industry.
Queue forming outside Port Newark.
Delays are especially damaging to truck drivers, often paid per trip completed, rather than hourly.

Three years ago, the Port of Los Angeles established a Cybersecurity Operations Center, which has since handled 20 million-plus cyber intrusion attempts. Meanwhile, cyber-security experts have shown that it is possible to hijack a billionaire's superyacht from your laptop.

Clearly the industry's gaping security holes won't be fixed by a Windows XP update.

From the editor
We published a comment piece in Nature last week which I am super excited about: Protect the neglected half of our blue planet. I am currently reading The Box by Marc Levinson. Malcom McLean, who's parents didn't have enough money to send him to university, used their meagre savings to buy him a truck. He turned that truck into a freight company, and ultimately pioneered the intermodal shipping container. There are now more than 20 million containers in circulation. The world's biggest container ship can carry 21,000 of them.
