"We need a radical transformation of the take-make-dispose plastics system 
towards one that is circular by design"
~ Ellen MacArthur
"Tesla of the Canals"
The world’s first fully electric, emissions-free container barges will transport goods around inland waterways of Belgium and the Netherlands this summer. They are designed to operate without a crew, and are expected to vastly reduce the use of diesel-powered trucks.
According to the latest statistics from Eurostat, only 6.7% of freight in the EU is transported by inland waterways, compared to 74.9% by road, and 18.4% by rail. These low-carbon vessels could revive our waterways - around 23,000 trucks, most of them diesel, are expected to be removed from the roads as a result of this initial deployment. 

While international shipping currently accounts for less than 3% of global greenhouse gas emissions, this figure is expected to grow as the burgeoning middle class drives huge increases in consumption. Yet shipping and aviation are exempt from the Paris Agreement and there are no solid targets for emissions reduction (partly because their emissions are hard to attribute to any single nation).

This could change in 2018, as the International Maritime Organisation plans to issue its long-awaited initial climate strategy. An ambitious strategy could set strong emissions goals for shipping, encouraging the development of an electric fleet and transforming the industry.
Gifs of the week

Youth campaigners of the Bahamas Plastic Movement convinced the government to ban plastic bags, much to the delight of the country's swimming pigs.
“If we can have rubbish bins on land, why not have them in the ocean”.
The Seabin Project installs floating bins in docks, yacht clubs and commercial ports.
Plastics: coming full circle
From beach clean-ups to #stopsucking, young people across the globe are taking action on plastics. But this crisis urgently needs solutions that address root causes, not just symptoms. Evoware, an Indonesian startup, designs edible food packaging made out of seaweed. Not only does this reduce waste, it could also be a boon for low-income seaweed-growers. It's a neat idea, which is why the founders just won $200,000 from the Ellen MacArthur Foundation's Circular Materials Challenge, announced this week at the World Economic Forum in Davos. Other winners include a "returnable cup ecosystem" and "plastic" packaging made from wood.
Meanwhile, on land
London has a serious plastic problem. It's residents throw away an incredible 7.7bn plastic bottles a year, accounting for 10% of all litter found in the Thames. Mayor Sadiq Khan has announced a scheme to improve access to drinking water in the city in a bid to reduce plastic waste. Twenty new drinking fountains will be installed and a bottle-refill initiative will be launched, encouraging businesses to make drinking water available. City Hall is also getting rid of plastic cups, bottles and cutlery.
From the editor
It's Friday, you deserve another swimming pig:

I had planned to rant about how Davos tokenism is itself a symptom of the root causes, but the swimming pigs melted my anger away. Here's a couple of interesting conservation conflicts instead: 1. The European Parliament has called for a moratorium on seabed mining, despite the fact that it forms part of the Commission's ambitious 'Blue Economy' plans. (No, I don't understand the difference between all these EU institutions either!); 2. The South Pacific Regional Fisheries Management Organisation (SPRFMO) meets next week. New Zealand is proposing new conservation measures to protect vulnerable marine ecosystems from destructive bottom trawling, but its fisherman are threatening to sue. Chile, also a member of SPRFMO has closed 98% of its waters to destructive bottom trawling.
