26 September 2018 — Deeply surprising

Created on September 26, 2018

Sent 26 September 2018. Part of Little Blue Letter.

"If the ocean can calm itself, so can you.
We are both salt water mixed with air."
~ Nayyirah Waheed
Deeply surprising
The Deep-Sea Biology Symposium took place in California last week, with researchers from around the world showcasing their latest finds. In areas once considered devoid of life or scientific interest, we now find "Gummy squirrels”, single-celled organisms the size of softballs, strange worms, fossil beds of whales that lived up to 16 million years ago, and an incredible array of fish and corals.
Red shrimp, a rattail fish and crustaceans called amphipods.
Credit: DeepCCZ project

The International Seabed Authority has already issued licenses to 29 corporations and state-backed companies to explore the deep sea for minerals and is aiming to have exploitation regulations in place by 2020 (developed through a notoriously secretive process). Researchers say that devastating impacts are inevitable and irrevocable, and many are now calling for a moratorium.
A Chaunacops anglerfish
Credit: NOAA Office of Ocean Exploration and Research
Ocean leadership
The UK announced this week that it will support a goal of protecting 30% of the global ocean, a target previously suggested as necessary by scientific studies. Elsewhere, the inaugural meeting of the High-level Panel on Building a Sustainable Ocean Economy took place. Norwegian Prime Minister Erna Solberg, whose country is spearheading the initiative, said: "As the only ocean policy body consisting of serving world leaders, we have the authority and determination needed to trigger, amplify and accelerate action for ocean protection and production... Our goal is to advance a new contract that will both protect the oceans and optimise their value to humankind."

From the editor
“The commercial fishing industry is losing the battle over the social license to operate... I believe fishing industries worldwide need to step forward and accept levels of transparency in fishing activities that were unimaginable a decade ago”, says Ray Hillborn, a leading (and sometimes controversial) fisheries scientist and professor at the University of Washington. The US Board on Geographic Names quietly decided to rename Antarctica’s Marchant Glacier after an investigation concluded that its namesake, Boston University geologist David Marchant, sexually harassed a former graduate student. Photos of bored tourists on their smartphones. The bridge that crossed an ocean (And the man who moved it). How corporations can lead on ocean science. A lovely long read about giant glowing clams.
