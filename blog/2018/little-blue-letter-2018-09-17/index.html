Back to Nature
French researchers searching for early warning systems on ocean pollution have found an unlikely ally in a mollusc that usually ends up on dinner plates. Like canaries in a coal mine, oysters living near offshore oil platforms can detect minute amounts of hydrocarbons in the dozens of litres of water they filter each day. Careful monitoring could alert scientists to tiny infrastructure cracks before they develop into catastrophic oil spills that threaten entire ecosystems and coastal communities.  
Yet global carbon emissions (which hit a record high in 2017) are contributing to the demise of mussels and other species. As temperatures continue to rise, water temperatures become too hot for mussels to survive, as has happened in southwestern France.
#OceanOptimism
Whales may be able to rest a little easier, for now at least, as Japan's attempt to overturn the decades-old global ban on commercial whaling failed during this year's meeting of the International Whaling Commission (IWC) in Brazil. Delegates adopted the Florianópolis Declaration on the Role of the IWC in the Conservation and Management of Whales in the 21st Century, which reaffirms the importance of maintaining a moratorium on commercial whaling and states that the use of lethal research methods is unnecessary.

Ready salted
Salt water is the most common substance on earth. There is 3.5 grams of salt per litre of open ocean water and the earliest evidence of salt processing dates to around 8,000 years ago; people living in Romania boiled spring water to extract salts and a salt processing site from this time has been identified in China. Salt is also vital for life as we know it, with diverse roles that range from driving crucial ocean currents to carrying the electrical signals that make our brains work.
