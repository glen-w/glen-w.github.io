Eyes on the Seas
Illegal fishing is big business, worth between $10 billion and US $23.5 billion annually. It is also an incredibly low risk crime. Despite increasing attention on monitoring, control and surveillance (MCS), illegal fishers are almost never caught; those that are rarely face meaningful penalties. (Except in Indonesia, where a seriously badass fisheries minister is blowing up boats.)
Indonesian Fisheries Minister Susi Pudjiastuti enjoying a cup of coffee and a cigarette on her paddleboard after a hard day's work blowing up illegal fishing vessels.

A number of initiatives are now underway that seek to reverse this trend. FISH-i Africa is bringing together countries to cooperate on fisheries enforcement and bring illegal vessels to justice. They are also supplementing information from vessels' automatic identification systems (AIS), originally designed to support safe navigation, with new tools that allow them to more effectively track fishing activity. (AIS is far from perfect though - it is possible for vessels to "go dark", as detailed in a new report out this week).

Ocean Mind is using machine learning and big data analytics to sift through tons of data (satellite observations, vessel tracking data and identity databases, fishing license information). Their algorithms pick out possible non-compliance so that analysts can investigate. Pew's Eyes on the Seas project is using the technology to develop a cost-effective monitoring and enforcement tool that will help governments, including the most resource-poor enforcement agencies, to clamp down on illegal fishing.
